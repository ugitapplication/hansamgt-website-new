Fire and life safety systems almost never fail all at once. They degrade quietly: a detector drifts out of calibration, a fire door gets wedged open for a delivery, an emergency light burns out, a drill is postponed because the building is busy. Each of those looks minor on its own. Together they are the difference between a system that works and a system that only appears to.

Below are seven failures that are easy to miss in commercial buildings — hotels, offices, hospitals, factories and residential towers — and what prevents them.

1. An alarm panel that has never been tested end to end

Panels get checked. What often does not get checked is the whole chain: a detector triggers the panel, the panel triggers the sounders, the sounders are audible where people actually are, and the signal reaches whoever is meant to respond. Testing each part separately can still leave a broken chain.

Prevention: test the full sequence on a schedule, including the areas nobody visits — plant rooms, service corridors, car parks, storage areas — and record the result, not just the fact that a test took place.

2. Detectors that have drifted out of calibration

Smoke and heat detectors accumulate dust, grease and humidity. A detector in a kitchen extract area or a dusty plant room works far harder than one in an office. Left alone, they either stop responding or start false alarming — and false alarms are how people learn to ignore real ones.

Prevention: clean and test each detector on the schedule the manufacturer sets, keep a detector-by-detector log with dates, and treat repeated false alarms as a maintenance fault rather than a nuisance.

3. Fire doors propped open

This is the most common failure, and the most human. A fire door held open by a chair, a brick or a piece of cardboard defeats the compartmentation it exists to provide. Self-closers get damaged and go unreported; hinges sag until the door never latches properly.

Prevention: inspect doors as part of a routine, not once a year. Check the closer, the latch, the gaps and the seals. Give staff a simple way to report a door that will not close — and a reason to care.

4. Escape routes used as storage

Corridors, stair landings and exits are convenient places to leave a trolley, a pallet or a stack of chairs "just for now". Nothing in the building is broken, but an exit that is blocked is an exit that does not exist.

Prevention: walk the escape routes as a routine. Name the person responsible for keeping them clear, and make clearance part of a daily shift check rather than an annual audit.

5. Emergency lighting with dead batteries

Emergency lights are invisible until the power fails — which is exactly why batteries go unreplaced. A test only matters if someone writes down what failed and replaces it.

Prevention: test emergency lighting on a schedule, log every failure with a date, and treat the log as a work queue rather than a record.

6. Extinguishers that are out of service or out of reach

Two quiet problems: an extinguisher past its service date, and an extinguisher that has been moved behind a rack or a display. In the moment it is needed, both are equally useless.

Prevention: keep a register of location, type and service date; check gauges and seals during routine inspections; and confirm that signage still matches reality after any layout change.

7. Drills that exist only on paper

A drill that is documented but never run teaches nobody anything. It also hides the real problems: a stairwell that bottlenecks, a muster point that is not where people believe it is, a fire warden who happens to be on leave.

Prevention: schedule drills, run them unannounced where appropriate, time the evacuation, and record what went wrong. The lesson is in the failure, not the certificate.

What ties all seven together

None of these failures require new equipment or a large budget. They require an asset register, a schedule, a named owner, and the habit of writing down what was found and fixed.

That is the part of fire and life safety that is genuinely hard to buy: not the alarm panel, but the discipline of the person who checks it on a Tuesday morning and escalates the door that will not close.
