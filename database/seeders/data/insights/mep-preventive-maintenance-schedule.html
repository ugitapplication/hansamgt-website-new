Most MEP failures are not sudden. A pump seizes after months of a worn seal nobody replaced. A chiller trips in the hottest week of the year because filters were never changed. A generator fails its first real test because it has only ever been started and stopped.

Reactive maintenance looks cheaper because the cost arrives later, in a different budget line, at the worst possible moment. Planned preventive maintenance is simply the discipline of deciding in advance what gets done, how often, and by whom.

Step 1 — Build an asset register

You cannot maintain what you have not listed. The register does not need to be a design document; it needs to be complete enough to schedule work against. For each asset, capture:

  • What it is and where it is — make, model, capacity, location and a tag number
  • What it serves — which floors, rooms or processes depend on it
  • What the manufacturer requires — service intervals, spares, warranty conditions
  • Its condition, age and last known service date

Walk the building with a notepad. The gaps in a register are usually the systems nobody has thought about since hand-over.

Step 2 — Rank by criticality, not by size

A small booster pump can matter more than a large chiller if it serves a kitchen, a firefighting system or a data room. Sort assets into tiers — what stops the building, what degrades comfort, what can wait — and let the schedule follow that order.

Step 3 — Set the frequencies

The manufacturer's schedule is the starting point, and normally overrides everything else. Where there is no guidance, frequency follows three things: how hard the equipment works, how hostile the environment is, and how bad the consequence of failure would be.

Where a manufacturer's schedule was written for a mild, dry climate, it is worth reviewing against local conditions. In Bangladesh that usually means humidity and dust, water quality and scale in cooling and domestic water systems, and heavy reliance on standby power.

Step 4 — Turn it into a schedule someone owns

A preventive maintenance list only becomes real when it has dates, names and completion records. The practical essentials:

  • A rolling twelve-month calendar covering daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly and annual tasks
  • One named person accountable for the programme, and a named person for each task
  • Job cards or digital work orders with a signature or time stamp
  • A backlog list reviewed every month — not a list that quietly grows

Step 5 — Cover the systems that fail quietly

Some equipment announces its failure. These do not:

  • Water treatment. Scale, corrosion and biological growth in cooling towers and closed loops, invisible until efficiency collapses or equipment is destroyed.
  • Drainage and sumps. Blocked floor drains, silted sumps and failed non-return valves reveal themselves only in a flood.
  • Standby power. Generators, fuel quality, batteries and automatic transfer switches need real load tests, not just starts.
  • Fire pumps and valves. A pump that has never run, or a valve left closed, is a system that will not work when it must.
  • Lifts and building management systems. Both degrade gracefully, and are noticed only when a waiting time or a control failure becomes a complaint.

Step 6 — Review with real data

Once you have a year of records, the questions change. Which assets failed despite being serviced? Which tasks were repeatedly postponed? Where are we spending time that produces nothing?

A schedule that is never reviewed becomes a ritual. A schedule that is reviewed gets cheaper every year, because failures start being caught before the breakdown rather than after it.
